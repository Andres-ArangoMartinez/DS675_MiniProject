Para este proyecto, en **Kaggle debes buscar principalmente un dataset real con el que puedas plantear un problema de Machine Learning**. Revisé el syllabus de DS675: ahí se indica que el Mini-Project es un proyecto de implementación de ML dividido en cuatro milestones, pero **no especifica en ese PDF un dataset concreto ni requisitos como número mínimo de filas o columnas**.

Para facilitarte el proyecto durante todo el semestre, yo buscaría un dataset de **clasificación supervisada**, idealmente **clasificación binaria**. Es la opción más cómoda porque podrás aplicar y comparar muchos de los algoritmos que aparecen en el curso: k-NN, perceptron, logistic regression, SVM, decision trees, random forests, boosting y eventualmente neural networks.

### Qué debería tener el dataset

Busca algo que cumpla aproximadamente esto:

- **Formato:** CSV.
- **Filas:** idealmente entre **2,000 y 50,000+ observaciones**.
- **Features:** unas **8–30 columnas** es una cantidad cómoda.
- **Target claro:** una columna que quieras predecir.
  - `0 / 1`
  - `Yes / No`
  - `Fraud / Not Fraud`
  - `Default / No Default`
  - `Disease / No Disease`
- Varias variables numéricas y, si es posible, algunas categóricas.
- Que no sea tan pequeño que los modelos sean triviales.
- Que tampoco sea gigantesco o dependa de imágenes, audio o texto complejo.
- Preferiblemente que tenga algún reto interesante: valores faltantes, clases desbalanceadas, necesidad de feature engineering, etc.

Por ejemplo, un dataset con:

```text
Age
Income
CreditScore
LoanAmount
EmploymentYears
DebtRatio
MaritalStatus
Education
...
Loan_Default   <-- TARGET
```

sería **muy apropiado para DS675**.

Evitaría por ahora datasets de **imágenes**, NLP/texto, series temporales complicadas o datasets extremadamente conocidos y pequeños como Iris, porque pueden darte poco espacio para desarrollar un proyecto de un semestre.

Sí. De hecho, puedes convertirlo en un proyecto bastante más interesante si planteas **dos objetivos relacionados dentro de una red de distribución de agua potable**:

1. **Detección de fugas o condiciones anormales.**
2. **Predicción/monitoreo de la presión en un nodo remoto**, con la idea de apoyar posteriormente el control de presión.

Hay una diferencia importante: **predecir la presión** y **controlar la presión** no son exactamente lo mismo. Para hablar de control necesitas que el dataset incluya también variables que puedas manipular, como estado/velocidad de bombas, apertura de válvulas o setpoints.

### Cómo se vería tu problema

Imagina una red así:

```text
Tank / Pump
     |
     ↓
 [Valve]
     |
     ↓
----Network----------------------Remote Node
   P1    P2    Flow                P_remote
```

Tu modelo podría aprender:

\[
P_{\text{remote}} =
f(
P_1,
P_2,
Flow,
Demand,
TankLevel,
PumpStatus,
ValveStatus,
Time,
...
)
\]

Por ejemplo, tus features podrían ser:

```text

Pump_Status

Water_Demand
Hour
Day_of_week
```

y tu target:

```text
Pressure_Remote_Node
```

Eso es un problema de **regresión**, porque la presión es una variable continua.

Por ejemplo:



Ese segundo problema se parece mucho más a lo que estás describiendo.

# 16. Hay un detalle técnico que sí tendremos que cuidar

Ambos son **time-series datasets**.

Por eso, cuando llegue el momento de entrenamiento/test, yo no haría automáticamente:

```python
train_test_split(..., shuffle=True)
```

como si las observaciones fueran completamente independientes.

Si tienes:

```text
10:00
10:05
10:10
10:15
```

y pones 10:00 y 10:10 en training y 10:05 en test, el modelo puede recibir información extremadamente parecida a la observación que intenta predecir.

Sería más serio hacer algo como:

```text
January ───────── September  → TRAIN
October ───────── November   → VALIDATION
December                     → TEST
```

o aprovechar los periodos de entrenamiento/test ya definidos.

Esto además te da material excelente para Milestone 2: **data preparation, leakage prevention, feature engineering y validación**, que es precisamente el tipo de trabajo práctico que el profesor dijo que espera que descubran durante el proyecto. fileciteturn6file7

# Milestone 1 – Candidate Datasets and Predictive Learning Tasks

## General Area of Interest

My general area of interest is pressure management in drinking-water distribution systems, particularly the monitoring and regulation of pressure at remote critical nodes.

This project is motivated by previous work on remote-node pressure regulation in a real water distribution network. In that work, field measurements of inlet flow and pressure were used to identify a low-order dynamic model relating system operation to pressure at a remote critical node. The identified model was later used for controller design and was able to reproduce remote-node pressure with approximately ±1 m H₂O error on independent validation data. fileciteturn13file0

For this machine learning project, I would like to explore whether supervised learning algorithms can predict pressure at a remote or critical node directly from SCADA measurements. In addition to pressure prediction, I am interested in studying whether abnormal operating conditions, such as leakage or cyber-physical disturbances, affect prediction performance.

The two candidate datasets below provide different opportunities for investigating this problem.

---

# Candidate Dataset 1: BattLeDIM

**Dataset:** Battle of the Leakage Detection and Isolation Methods (BattLeDIM)

urlBattLeDIM on Kagglehttps://www.kaggle.com/datasets/larserikrisholm/battledim

urlOfficial BattLeDIM dataset and documentationhttps://zenodo.org/records/4017659

## Dataset Description

BattLeDIM is a benchmark dataset developed for the detection and localization of leakage events in the L-Town water distribution network.

The dataset contains SCADA measurements for 2018 and 2019 at **5-minute intervals**. Measurements include **33 pressure sensors, 3 flow sensors, 1 tank-level sensor, and 82 automated meter readings (AMRs) representing aggregated customer consumption**. The official dataset also contains leakage-event information and an EPANET representation of the network. citeturn964120search0turn964120search1

A complete year sampled every five minutes provides approximately:

\[
365\times24\times12 \approx 105,120
\]

time observations.

This gives the dataset substantially more observations than input variables, which makes it appropriate for experimenting with multiple supervised learning approaches.

## Proposed Predictive Task

The primary task would be **regression**.

The objective would be to predict pressure at a selected remote critical node a short time into the future:

\[
\boxed{
P_{\text{critical}}(t+15\text{ min})
}
\]

using measurements available at time \(t\) and, potentially, measurements from previous time steps.

Conceptually:

\[
X(t)
=
[
\text{pressures},
\text{flows},
\text{tank level},
\text{customer demand},
\text{time information}
]
\]

and:

\[
y=P_{\text{critical}}(t+15\text{ min})
\]

The particular pressure sensor used as the critical node would not be selected arbitrarily. It would be identified after examining the network topology, node elevations, and observed pressure conditions.

The 15-minute horizon is an initial proposal. Because measurements are available every 5 minutes, predictions at 5, 15, or 30 minutes ahead could later be compared.

## Possible Input Features

Potential predictors include pressure measurements from other parts of the network, DMA flow measurements, tank level, AMR consumption measurements, time of day and day of week, and lagged measurements from previous time steps.

Feature selection would be investigated later rather than manually choosing a small subset at the beginning. This is consistent with the course emphasis on treating feature selection and dimensionality reduction as part of the exploratory process. fileciteturn12file0

## Relation to the Previous Pressure-Control Work

This dataset has a particularly strong connection to my previous work.

The previous study used 5-minute historical measurements of inlet flow and pressure to characterize pressure at a remote critical node. BattLeDIM also provides measurements every five minutes, but contains a substantially larger and richer sensor network. fileciteturn13file0

The proposed ML problem can therefore be viewed as an extension of the previous idea:

\[
\text{Hydraulic measurements}
\rightarrow
\text{dynamic model}
\rightarrow
P_{\text{remote}}
\]

becomes:

\[
\text{SCADA measurements}
\rightarrow
\text{ML model}
\rightarrow
\hat P_{\text{remote}}
\]

Instead of imposing a particular low-order dynamic structure, the machine-learning algorithms would learn the predictive relationship from the data.

## Potential Original Contribution

The original BattLeDIM benchmark focuses primarily on **leak detection and localization**. citeturn964120search1turn964120search2

I would instead use **remote pressure forecasting as the primary predictive task**.

The leakage information could then provide a secondary research question:

> Does the accuracy of a remote-pressure prediction model deteriorate when a leakage event occurs?

For example, prediction errors could eventually be compared under:

\[
RMSE_{\text{normal}}
\]

and

\[
RMSE_{\text{leak}}
\]

This would connect pressure management and leakage behavior without simply reproducing the original BattLeDIM leak-detection competition.

## Limitation

BattLeDIM contains excellent pressure, flow, consumption, and leakage information, but does not provide the same rich set of explicit pump and valve control states available in BATADAL.

Therefore, I would describe the project as **remote pressure prediction or pressure-management support**, rather than claiming that the dataset by itself supports direct automatic pressure control.

# Candidate Dataset 2: BATADAL

**Dataset:** BATADAL – Cyber Attacks Detection in Water Systems

urlBATADAL on Kagglehttps://www.kaggle.com/datasets/minhbtnguyen/batadal-a-dataset-for-cyber-attack-detection

urlOfficial BATADAL datasethttps://www.batadal.net/data.html

## Dataset Description

BATADAL is based on the C-Town water distribution system and was originally developed for detecting cyber-physical attacks on water infrastructure.

The SCADA dataset contains operational variables associated with **7 tanks, 11 pumps, one actuated valve, pump and valve flow rates, actuator states, and pressure measurements at 24 locations associated with pumps and the actuated valve**. The Kaggle version describes 43 SCADA variables together with attack labels. citeturn768260search0

En el contexto de **BattLeDIM**, probablemente te refieres a los **AMR**, no “ARM”.

**AMR** significa **Automatic Meter Reading**. Son medidores de consumo de agua que registran automáticamente el consumo y permiten enviar esas lecturas sin necesidad de que una persona vaya físicamente a leer el medidor.

En BattLeDIM, los **82 AMR demands** representan lecturas de demanda/consumo asociadas a distintos puntos o grupos de usuarios de la red. En términos de ML, esas variables son muy útiles porque ayudan a explicar por qué cambia la presión.

Por ejemplo, conceptualmente:

\[
\text{Demand} \uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{Flow} \uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{Head loss} \uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{Pressure at remote node} \downarrow
\]

Entonces, para tu proyecto, los AMR podrían ser parte de las **features** usadas para predecir la presión futura del nodo remoto.

En tu tabla, algo como:

```text
AMR demands (82)
n1, n2, n3, ...
```

significa que tienes **82 series temporales de consumo/demanda**.

Si quieres, también puedo explicarte qué significan exactamente en BattLeDIM las categorías **Pressure, Flow, Tank level y AMR demand**, y cómo se relacionarían con `X` y `y` en tu modelo.
