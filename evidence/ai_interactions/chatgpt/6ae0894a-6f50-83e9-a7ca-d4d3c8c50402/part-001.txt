My selection of Area C was guided by an engineering assessment of the available hydraulic measurements and operating conditions across the three sectors of the L-Town network.

Area A initially appeared attractive because it contains the largest number of pressure measurements. However, its supply passes through two pressure-reducing valves (PRVs), and its pressure behavior can be influenced by demand variations, seasonal consumption patterns, and unpredictable changes in water use. Area B also has a regulated supply boundary but offers only one measured pressure node, limiting the possibility of studying relationships between multiple pressure measurements.

Area C presented a different and particularly interesting configuration. Rather than being supplied directly through a pressure-regulating valve, it receives water from tank T1, whose level varies with the balance between pump inflow and sector consumption. The available measurements include tank level, pump flow, and three pressure sensors (n1, n4, and n31). This configuration motivated my hypothesis that the hydraulic state of the tank, together with pressure measurements at nearby nodes, might provide useful information for forecasting future pressure.

I selected n1 as the target because it has both the highest elevation and the longest pipe-path distance from T1 among the three monitored pressure nodes in Area C. These characteristics make it an interesting candidate for studying pressure sensitivity, although they do not independently establish it as the hydraulically most critical node in the sector.

I also included AMR_n1, the local water-consumption measurement, to investigate whether demand information contributes predictive value beyond pressure history and the other hydraulic signals.

This approach builds on my previous research experience in dynamic pressure regulation in real water distribution networks (Arango-Martinez et al., 2026). In that work, pressure and flow measurements supported dynamic system identification and pressure-control design at a remote critical node. My earlier experience also motivated the use of superimposed daily time-series profiles and their mean curves to examine recurring patterns and operational variability.

The contribution proposed here is not a new forecasting algorithm, but a controlled investigation of whether physically motivated measurements provide incremental predictive information for 15-minute-ahead pressure forecasting. The experiments will test this hypothesis rather than assume that the additional variables necessarily improve prediction.
